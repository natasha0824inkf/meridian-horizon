UAE Mainland vs Free Zone: Which Structure is Right for Your Business?

One of the most common questions we receive from entrepreneurs and investors looking to set up in the UAE is a simple one: mainland or free zone? The answer is rarely simple, and the wrong choice can create friction down the line - with banking, with clients, with licensing, or with future growth plans.

This article sets out the key differences between the two structures and the factors that should drive your decision. For the banking implications of each structure, see our guide to opening a business bank account in the UAE.

What is a UAE Mainland Company?

A mainland company is licensed by the relevant emirate's Department of Economic Development (DED) - for example, the Dubai DED or Abu Dhabi DED. A mainland licence allows you to:

  • Trade directly with the UAE market and government entities without restrictions
  • Operate from any commercial premises in the emirate
  • Bid for government and semi-government contracts
  • Have an unlimited number of visas (subject to office space requirements)
  • Branch out into any emirate without a separate licence

Historically, mainland companies required a UAE national sponsor holding 51% of the shares. Since the 2021 Foreign Direct Investment law amendments, most commercial activities now allow 100% foreign ownership on the mainland - a significant change that has made mainland formation considerably more attractive to international investors.

What is a UAE Free Zone Company?

Free zones are special economic zones - each managed by its own authority - that offer specific benefits to attract foreign businesses. There are over 40 free zones across the UAE, each targeting different industries. DIFC, ADGM, DMCC, IFZA, and Meydan are among the most widely used.

A free zone company can:

  • Be 100% foreign-owned (always, regardless of the 2021 reforms)
  • Benefit from corporate tax exemptions in many zones (note: UAE corporate tax now applies across the board at 9% above AED 375,000 profits)
  • Operate with simplified setup processes and lower initial costs in some zones
  • Benefit from industry-specific infrastructure and a credible regulatory environment (especially DIFC and ADGM for financial services)

The key restriction: free zone companies cannot trade directly on the UAE mainland without a mainland licence or a local distribution arrangement. This is the most common cause of mismatch between structure and business model.

The Critical Question: Who Are Your Clients?

The decision often comes down to one question: where are your clients, and how will you invoice them?

If your business model involves selling to UAE-based companies or consumers, a mainland licence is usually more appropriate. If you operate primarily internationally - billing clients outside the UAE, running an e-commerce operation, or managing a holding structure - a free zone may be sufficient and more cost-effective.

That said, some free zones do allow B2B trading on the mainland through specific commercial agreements, and some business models operate quite effectively through a free zone entity with a separate mainland distribution arrangement. The specifics matter.

Banking Implications

Banking is a practical consideration that is often overlooked at the structuring stage. UAE banks generally have a preference for mainland-licensed entities. Free zone companies - especially from newer or smaller free zones - can face more scrutiny during account opening. DIFC and ADGM entities tend to be well-regarded by banks, partly due to their regulatory standing.

If banking is a priority concern, this should be part of the structure conversation from day one. See our detailed guide to UAE business banking requirements for what banks look for and how to prepare.

Costs and Setup Time

Free zone setup costs vary significantly. Some zones (such as IFZA or Meydan) have become competitive on price, with packages starting well under AED 15,000. More prestigious zones like DIFC carry higher entry costs but come with corresponding benefits in terms of regulatory recognition and banking accessibility.

Mainland licences typically cost more upfront, particularly when factoring in tenancy requirements (Ejari), but this varies by emirate and activity.

Which Should You Choose?

There is no universal answer. The right structure depends on:

  • Your industry and the specific activity you are licensed for
  • Who your clients are and where they are based
  • Your banking requirements
  • Your visa requirements
  • Your medium-term expansion plans
  • Your budget for setup and ongoing costs

A conversation with an advisor who understands the full picture - not just one part of it - is essential before committing to a structure. You may also want to read our guide to corporate structuring in the UAE for context on how holding structures and offshore layers fit in.
