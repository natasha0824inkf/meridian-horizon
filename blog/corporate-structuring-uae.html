Corporate Structuring in the UAE: A Practical Guide for International Investors

The UAE offers a flexible corporate environment with multiple entity types, licensing jurisdictions and holding structures that can be combined to meet a wide range of operational and investment objectives. If you are still deciding between a mainland and free zone entity, start with our UAE mainland vs free zone comparison. For international investors in particular, getting the structure right from the start is important - restructuring later can be costly and complicated.

This article provides a practical overview of the main structural tools available in the UAE and the considerations that drive good structuring decisions.

The Building Blocks: Entity Types

UAE corporate structures typically draw on three types of entity:

Mainland Operating Companies

Licensed by an emirate's Department of Economic Development (DED), mainland companies can trade freely within the UAE. Since the 2021 foreign ownership reforms, most commercial activities allow 100% foreign ownership. These are the standard choice for businesses with UAE-based clients or operations.

Free Zone Companies

Each of the UAE's 40+ free zones operates under its own authority, offering varying benefits in terms of cost, industry focus, and regulatory prestige. Free zone companies are 100% foreign-owned and can hold assets, employ staff, and invoice internationally - but typically cannot trade directly on the UAE mainland without a separate arrangement. The DIFC and ADGM stand apart as full legal jurisdictions with their own courts and regulatory frameworks, making them the preferred choice for financial services, fund structures and high-value holding arrangements.

Offshore Companies

UAE offshore companies (available in RAK ICC, JAFZA Offshore and others) are not operational entities - they do not provide visas or allow UAE-based commercial activity. They are designed for asset holding, international trading, and as intermediate holding layers within a wider corporate structure.

Common Structural Approaches

Simple Operating Structure

For many businesses, a single mainland or free zone company is sufficient - an entity that holds the licence, employs the team, and conducts the business. This is appropriate for smaller businesses, early-stage operations, and those with straightforward activity profiles.

Holding and Operating Separation

Investors with multiple ventures in the UAE, or those seeking to separate asset ownership from operational risk, often use a holding company structure. A DIFC or ADGM holding company owning the shares of mainland or free zone operating subsidiaries is a common arrangement. This provides a clear layer of asset protection and facilitates investment structuring, shareholder arrangements, and eventual exit.

International Holding via Offshore

Investors holding UAE interests alongside other international assets may use an offshore or third-country holding vehicle above the UAE structure. This can assist with estate planning, international treaty benefits, and investment management. The appropriate choice of jurisdiction for the holding layer depends on the investor's tax residency, home country rules, and the nature of the underlying assets.

Tax Considerations

The UAE introduced a federal corporate tax of 9% on business profits exceeding AED 375,000 from June 2023. Free zone companies that meet qualifying income and substance requirements can access a 0% rate on qualifying income - but this requires careful structuring and ongoing compliance to maintain.

The UAE has an extensive treaty network. For investors from countries with which the UAE has a double taxation agreement, treaty benefits may apply to dividends, interest and capital gains flowing through UAE entities - though treaty shopping arrangements attract increasing regulatory scrutiny internationally.

What Good Structuring Looks Like

Good corporate structuring is not about finding the most complex arrangement - it is about building a structure that fits the actual business, can be maintained in practice, will be understood by banks, and positions the investor for growth without creating future complications.

The most common structuring mistakes we see are:

  • Choosing a free zone entity when the business model requires mainland trading
  • Adding unnecessary holding layers that create banking complexity without corresponding benefit
  • Setting up a structure that looks good on paper but cannot obtain a business bank account
  • Failing to consider substance requirements for corporate tax purposes
  • Not planning for shareholder exits or additions from the outset

A well-structured UAE corporate footprint requires input from legal, tax and commercial perspectives. The right structure for one business is wrong for another, and the specifics of the activity, the shareholders, the investor base and the long-term objectives all matter.
